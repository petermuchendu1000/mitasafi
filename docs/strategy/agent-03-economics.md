# The Economics of a Free Vacancy Directory — Where the Design's Equilibrium Differs From Its Intention

**Date: 2026-10-19**
**Agent 03 — market design / incentives review**

Tags: **[E]** = published evidence found. **[R]** = reasoned from stated assumptions. Treat **[R]**
as a claim to test, not a fact.

---

## The finding to argue with first

**Everything this directory can give a tenant is worth about 3–5% of their monthly rent. A rent
rise of 4% cancels all of it. And the same list that tells a tenant what a house costs tells a
landlord what the building next door is charging — so the project's own success is the most likely
source of that 4%.**

The agent's fee looks like the prize. It isn't: it is roughly the market price of a service the
landlord genuinely values (not leaving a unit empty), it mostly relocates rather than disappears, and
amortised over a tenancy it is small. The real asset you are building is not cheaper search. It is
the only honest record in Kenya of **what rent people actually pay, and that a given unit and
landlord are real** — the missing input for the thing that actually binds this household: the
KSh 36,000 it cannot raise to move.

Design rule that follows: **the seeker sees many prices; the landlord sees one.**

## 1. Where does the rent go?

### Assumptions (change these and the answer changes)

| Label | Assumption | Value |
|---|---|---|
| A1 | Target rent | KSh 10,000/month |
| A2 | Fares + wasted time per search | KSh 1,500 |
| A3 | Viewing fees paid across 3–6 viewings | KSh 2,250 |
| A4 | Agent commission when paid | 1 month = KSh 10,000 |
| A5 | Probability a tenant in this band pays full commission | 50% → expected KSh 5,000 |
| A6 | Average tenancy length | 30 months **[R]** — a 2025 report says many Kenyan renters stay 3–8 years ([african-realestate.com](https://www.african-realestate.com/press/high-deposits-are-pushing-middleincome-kenyans-towards-longterm-renting)) |

**Cost of finding and entering a house: 1,500 + 2,250 + 5,000 = KSh 8,750 per move.** Amortised:
8,750 / 30 = **KSh 292/month = 2.9% of rent.** At full commission, KSh 13,750 → **4.6%**. That band,
3% to 5%, is the entire prize.

### The three shillings are not the same shilling

- **Fares and time (KSh 1,500): burned.** Nobody receives it. Remove the friction and this money is
  *created*, not transferred. **It is the only part of the saving that cannot relocate, because there
  is no incumbent recipient to fight for it.**
- **Viewing fees (KSh 2,250) and commission (KSh 5,000): transfers** to agents and caretakers.

### Does the transfer reach the tenant?

Transparency creates surplus (the burned fares, plus filled void weeks — Q6). Who gets it is set by
**bargaining power**, which is set by the ratio of seekers to vacancies — and the directory does not
change that ratio by one unit. **[R]** With several seekers per vacancy, the short side
(landlord/caretaker) captures most of any new surplus because the long side competes it away.

Five relocation routes, by how easily they absorb KSh 5,000–8,000:

1. **Caretaker charges directly (easiest).** Removing the agent removes the caretaker's *supplier*,
   not the caretaker — he still holds the keys, the gate and the water. 28% of Kibera landlords
   already use intermediaries to collect rent ([Gulyani & Talukdar](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-1-4020-8862-9_8)) **[E]**:
   the collection layer exists and is already monetised.
2. **Inflated or extra deposit.** Kenya caps residential deposits for most lettings not at all, and
   practice already runs one to two months plus separate water and electricity deposits
   ([real.co.ke](https://www.real.co.ke/rent-deposit-disputes-kenya/)) **[E]**. It is also the item
   most often never refunded ([Nation](https://nation.africa/kenya/life-and-style/dn2/money-hungry-landlords-fleece-tenants-of-their-deposit-refunds-886648)) **[E]**.
3. **Goodwill / "commitment fee"** — already the commonest fraud here, now the legitimate
   replacement for the viewing fee.
4. **Service charge padding** — easy wherever the charge is unitemised.
5. **Landlord raises rent** (slowest, most durable). Needs him to learn the market clears higher.
   See Q2: you are about to teach him.

**Yes, there is a version where the tenant pays the same and only the recipient changes.** It is the
*default* version. The tenant's reliable gain is the KSh 1,500 of fares, because that is the only
line with no incumbent claimant. The KSh 7,250 of fees is contested, and the contest is between a
landlord who sets the price and a tenant who is one of five applicants.

## 2. Does transparency raise rents? Probably yes, and it only needs to be a little

### What the evidence says

- **Publishing prices in a concentrated market raised them 15–20% in one year.** Denmark's
  competition authority published firm-level transaction prices for ready-mixed concrete in 1993 to
  help buyers; prices of the published grades rose 15–20% within a year, because publication let
  firms soften price competition ([Albæk, Møllgaard & Overgaard, *J. Ind. Econ.* 1997](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/1467-6451.00057);
  [working version](https://sites.duke.edu/collardwexler/files/2015/01/danish_concrete_cartel.pdf)) **[E]**.
  The single most relevant empirical result in this report.
- **Shared landlord price information is now an antitrust theory, not a hypothetical.** DOJ and eight
  state AGs sued RealPage in 2024 alleging its software let landlords coordinate rents with no explicit
  agreement; the settlement bars runtime use of competitors' non-public data
  ([DOJ](https://justice.gov/archives/opa/pr/justice-department-sues-realpage-algorithmic-pricing-scheme-harms-millions-american-renters);
  [Mintz](https://www.mintz.com/insights-center/viewpoints/2191/2025-12-01-last-years-rent-realpage-reaches-settlement-agreement)).
  A Berkeley study estimated an association with higher rents of ~$0.23/sq ft/month
  ([Berkeley](https://www.ischool.berkeley.edu/projects/2025/investigation-alleged-rental-price-fixing-algorithmic-collusion-realpage-and-other)) **[E]**.
- **Theory does not promise cheaper search lowers prices.** The Diamond paradox: even a tiny search
  cost pushes a market to the monopoly price, and several later models find prices *rise* when search
  costs fall ([Moraga-González et al.](https://host.kelley.iu.edu/mwildenb/heterogeneity.pdf)) **[E]**.
  Where rental transparency was actually tested — California's AB 537 all-in pricing — guests became
  more price-sensitive and rates still rose 4.8%
  ([ICLE](https://laweconcenter.org/resources/clearing-the-fog-price-transparency-market-churn-and-consumer-welfare-in-short-term-rentals/)) **[E, weak]**.

### What the evidence says against me — state it honestly

- **Cheap listings demonstrably improve matching.** Kroft and Pope, using Craigslist's staggered
  entry, found rental vacancy rates fell ~11% (1.2pp) — while warning the welfare effects are less
  clear ([*JOLE*](http://www.korykroft.com/files/Kroft%20and%20Pope%20Manuscript.pdf)) **[E]**. No
  rent effect established either way.
- **Concentrating listing information may *lower* rents.** A Penn dissertation on Manhattan rental
  brokers finds larger brokers associated with lower rents and shorter listing times, and that
  *expanding* brokerage entry could hurt tenants by fragmenting listings
  ([Penn IUR](https://penniur.upenn.edu/publications/housing-search-and-rental-market-intermediation)) **[E]**
  — read directly, an argument *for* one big directory.
- Farboodi, Jarosch & Menzio find any equilibrium with rent-extracting intermediation is
  inefficient ([paper](https://web-facstaff.sas.upenn.edu/~gmenzio/linkies/FJM.pdf)) **[E]** — so
  killing the agent is in principle welfare-improving.

### Which effect dominates

Pipeline has thousands of small landlords, so a Nairobi-wide cartel is impossible. But that is the
wrong market definition. **[R]** The relevant market is *bedsitters within walking distance of one
matatu stage* — maybe 15 buildings and six caretakers who already know each other. Six players is
exactly cartel-sized, and Danish concrete needed no agreement, only published prices.

**Arithmetic of dominance:** benefit from cheaper search = KSh 8,750 one-off (Q1) = **KSh 292/month**.
Harm from a 4% rise on KSh 10,000 = **KSh 400/month**, or KSh 12,000 over 30 months. **A 4% rent rise
more than cancels the entire search benefit, and the Danish effect was 15–20% — you need one-fifth of
it to leave tenants worse off in cash.** **[R]**

**Conclusion:** net-positive on search cost with reasonable confidence; the price channel is larger
and currently unmanaged. Fixable by design, not hope: give the seeker price information, deny the
landlord *comparables*. No public, browsable, sortable rent-by-estate feed; prices visible only on
units a signed-in seeker is considering; rate-limited; any index published in arrears and coarse.
Secondary gain: nobody has reliable Kenyan rent data — published Nairobi yields conflict between
6.1% and 7.4% and vacancy estimates between 15% and 25%
([Cytonn via realtors.co.ke](https://realtors.co.ke/nairobi-rental-market-trends-2026/)) **[E]**.
That scarcity is your asset; publishing it live hands it to the wrong side.

## 3. Is the commons stable as it gets valuable?

### The attacker's arithmetic

Assumptions: **B1** an estate's listings are seen by 1,000 seekers/month at scale; **B2** an attacker
posting fakes with his own number as "caretaker" converts 5% to a paid viewing at KSh 1,000 →
**KSh 50,000/month**; **B3** cost of attack = a few SIM cards and an afternoon ≈ **KSh 500**.

**Attack return ≈ 100:1.** The defenders are two unpaid volunteers per building whose return is
reputation and whose cost includes conflict with the caretaker — who may be the attacker, or his
partner.

### The equilibrium is captured, not poisoned

**[R]** Poisoning is loud and self-defeating — fakes get reported and the attacker loses the channel.
**Capture is quiet and profitable.** A caretaker who pays a block rep KSh 1,000/month for first look,
or simply *becomes* the block rep, gets a laundered high-trust channel. Every listing stays true; the
rep just routes the early view to whoever pays. The stable state is **the current agent system with
better software and your brand on it.** India's NoBroker is the cautionary case: built to remove
brokers, and users now report paid owner contacts connecting them to brokers acting as "relationship
managers" ([review](https://www.nestriqo.com/blog/nobroker-owner-listings-review);
[comparison](https://manikarthik.com/blog/nobroker-vs-magicbricks)) **[E, weak — blogs/app reviews]**.

### The design bug that makes this worse

Ghosh and McAfee's model predicts that **if exposure is independent of quality, you get a flood of
low-quality content** ([paper](http://www.arpitaghosh.com/papers/nberfr320-ghosh.pdf)) **[E]**. Your
reward is conditioned on **contributing**, not on **being right** — so it pays for volume. And the
reputation literature names your missing ingredient exactly: there is *no ground truth against which
contributions are compared* ([CACM](https://cacm.acm.org/research/reputation-systems-for-open-collaboration/)) **[E]**.

### The mechanism that holds the good equilibrium

You can manufacture ground truth. **The post-move-in audit is the whole answer.** Ask whoever moved
in three questions — was it empty, was the price right, *did anyone ask you for money* — and trace
the answer back to whoever posted and confirmed. That one loop turns confirmations into falsifiable
claims rather than votes; lets you pay only on a *verified* let (a KSh 20–50 bounty per real move-in
costs almost nothing and inverts the attacker's economics — fakes earn zero and burn standing); and
detects capture, because a captured listing still produces a tenant who was asked for money.

Add two cheap rules: each building has a **phone number of record** confirmed by independent
residents, and any listing with a different contact is auto-flagged; and confirmation requires two
unrelated accounts that **predate the listing by 30+ days**, making sockpuppets expensive in time
rather than money.

## 4. "Time as payment" does not survive contact with timing

**[R]** The reward is a 6–12 hour head start. Its value = P(searching right now) × face value.
Assumptions: **C1** tenancy = 30 months (A6), so a resident is searching in roughly 1 month in 30 →
**3.3%** of months; **C2** a contributor who just moved in has P(searching within 7 days) ≈ **0**.

**You are paying people in a currency worth ~3% of face value at the instant you hand it over, at
the single worst moment in the 30-month cycle.** It also cannot be stored: a 6-hour head start on a
listing that decays in 7 days has no shelf life. This is the clearest *earn-now, need-later* mismatch
in the design.

Fixes, ranked:

1. **Make it giftable, not personal.** Three named, monthly-expiring passes you can give to a cousin
   or workmate. This matches the real motive — everyone gets asked *"unajua nyumba?"* — and turns a
   near-worthless private good into a valuable social one. Keep them non-fungible, expiring and
   non-stackable, so a pass worth KSh 200–400 is too small to build a business on.
2. **Bank it for the next move.** Credit redeemable in 30 months for things worth money *then*: a
   discounted mover, a rent-payment history certificate, and above all better terms on a moving loan
   (Q7). Carrying cost is low precisely because only ~3% redeem per month.
3. **Standing now.** Visible status pays immediately, is strictly better than the head start, and
   makes it largely redundant.

**Does the head start recreate the agent?** Not by resale — 6–12 hours is too thin to sell. The
quieter arbitrage is worse: the design makes a resident the **gatekeeper of their own building**,
which is the caretaker's job description. That is not a leak in the mechanism; it is how the agent
class reproduces. Capped passes plus rewards contingent on verified move-ins (Q3) contain it.

## 5. The verifier's incentive is negative — the worst problem in the design

Check each claimed benefit:

- **"Service charge is split fewer ways."** **[R]** In this band service charge is usually bundled
  into rent or flat per unit, not divided among occupants. If so, filling a unit saves the resident
  **nothing**. Twenty phone calls settles it.
- **"Better security."** Real, tiny, diffuse — a textbook under-supplied public good.
- **"A neighbour you helped choose."** **The mechanism does not deliver this.** The resident supplies
  applicants; the *landlord* selects. No selection right at all.
- **Shared amenities.** At the bottom, with shared taps and toilets, an empty unit is a **benefit**:
  more water pressure, shorter queues. **[R]** The incentive flips negative exactly where the housing
  need is greatest.

The cost side, which the design does not price: the caretaker loses the viewing fee, knows who took
it from him, and controls water, the gate, whether complaints reach the landlord, and **the deposit
refund** — KSh 10,000–20,000 of the resident's money, routinely withheld in practice
([Nation](https://nation.africa/kenya/life-and-style/dn2/money-hungry-landlords-fleece-tenants-of-their-deposit-refunds-886648)) **[E]**.

**So the resident risks KSh 10,000+ of hostage deposit to deliver roughly KSh 9,000 of value to the
landlord (Q6), for a reward worth 3% of a six-hour head start.** Expected value is negative for any
resident who thinks about it — and this sits **upstream of all the data**, which makes it more
serious than questions 2, 3 and 4 combined.

Free-riding is the lesser issue. **Retaliation** is the real one: free-riding means nobody reports;
retaliation means reporting is punished. Rational non-reporting: the resident fears the caretaker;
wants the unit for a relative (private information is worth more kept); is about to leave and does
not want the landlord to know; or simply prefers the unit empty.

**The fix is not a better reward. It is unattributability.** No "GG House reported by Mercy," no
public rep attached to a specific posting, confirmations aggregated and never individually visible,
volunteer roles rotated and detached from posting. A reward large enough to compensate a resident for
caretaker risk would have to exceed their deposit — which you cannot afford and should not want.

## 6. Who should actually pay

**Landlord's gain, quantified.** Assumptions: R = KSh 10,000; void cut 6 weeks → 2 weeks; 4.33
weeks/month; 20-unit building; 30-month tenancies → 8 turnovers/year.

- Rent recovered per let: 10,000 × (4 / 4.33) = **KSh 9,238**
- Per building per year: 8 × 9,238 = **KSh 73,900** = **3.1%** of gross annual rent (KSh 2.4m)

Cross-check: UK guidance suggests budgeting 3–5% of gross annual rent for voids
([Real Yield](https://www.realyield.co.uk/insights/void-periods-cost-and-how-to-reduce-them)) **[E]**.
3.1% lands inside it.

**Notice that KSh 9,238 is approximately one month's rent.** That is why the commission is one
month's rent and why it is so stable: **the agent's fee is priced at the value of the void it
prevents.** So removing the agent does not liberate a free month — only the gap between his fee and
his actual effort. **[R]**

**Ranking, by willingness to pay × whether the money corrupts the data:**

| Rank | Payer | WTP | Corrupts? | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **ISPs / connectivity** | KSh 1,500–3,000 per connected home **[R]** | No — cannot affect which listing a seeker sees | **Best fit** |
| 2 | **Aggregate rent data** (researchers, lenders, developers) | KSh 100k–1m/yr **[R]** | No — it is about the past | **Build toward this** |
| 3 | **Movers, boda, small trades** | KSh 100–200/referral | Low | Early cash, not scale |
| 4 | **SACCOs / deposit lenders** | High per customer | Only if they never see directory data and referrals are tenant-initiated | Conditional; strategically the most important (Q7) |
| 5 | **Landlords** | KSh 9,238 per let — **by far the highest** | **Yes, fatally.** A paying landlord buys placement, and placement kills the accuracy claim; also raises EARB exposure | Only as a flat, published, annual listed-accurately-and-nothing-else fee. Still risky |
| 6 | **Insurers** | Low in this band | — | Skip |
| 7 | **Counties as enforcers** | Real | **Refuse** — they want landlords identified for rates; landlords flee, tenants exposed | Refuse |

ISP arithmetic **[R, from [E] inputs]**: Safaricom had 1,024,950 fixed subscriptions by June 2026
([TechAfrica](https://techafricanews.com/2026/09/23/safaricom-crosses-1-million-fixed-internet-subscriptions-kenya/));
FTTH ARPU ≈ KSh 2,3xx and falling ([TechCabal](https://techcabal.com/2026/09/22/safaricom-hits-1-million-home-internet-users-now-comes-the-hard-part/));
Bronze KSh 2,999/month ([HapaKenya](https://hapakenya.com/2025/06/17/safaricom-home-fibre-prices-for-2025/)).
At 50% margin over 24 months, lifetime gross profit ≈ KSh 27,600; acquisition spend usually runs
10–25% of that → **KSh 2,700–6,900 per connection**. No published Kenyan CAC exists, so this is
inferred. Caveat: fibre take-up below KSh 10,000 rent is low, so the addressable slice is the top of
your range.

## 7. The deposit problem — you are solving the smaller one

Household: rent KSh 12,000. Assumptions: income KSh 26,000/month (rent = 46% of income, realistic at
the bottom); residual KSh 14,000; realistic saving **KSh 1,500/month**.

**Cash wall to move:**

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Deposit (1–2 months) + 1 month advance | KSh 36,000 |
| Agent commission | KSh 12,000 |
| Fares + viewing fees | KSh 3,750 |
| **Total** | **KSh 51,750** |

Time to save KSh 51,750 at KSh 1,500/month = **35 months.** Kenyan guides give a comparable all-in
figure of KSh 165,000–220,000 on a KSh 55,000 unit — the same 3–4 month ratio
([ksh.co.ke](https://ksh.co.ke/calculators/rent-affordability-calculator/)) **[E]**.

**What the directory removes:** KSh 3,750 of fares and fees **reliably** (7% of the wall), plus up to
KSh 12,000 of commission **if disintermediation holds** (23% more) — but Q1 says most of that
relocates to goodwill or an inflated deposit. **Realistic removal: 7–15%. The deposit and advance are
70% of the wall and are untouched.**

**The binding constraint is liquidity, not information.** A household that *knows* about a better
KSh 10,000 house and cannot raise KSh 30,000 is not helped by knowing. The recurring loss from being
stuck dwarfs the one-off search cost: overpaying KSh 2,000/month for a worse unit because you cannot
afford to move costs **KSh 24,000/year, every year** — more than the whole search cost, annually.
Your own first-principles doc says it: *"this is the real reason people stay in bad houses."*

**But search is the prerequisite.** Nobody lends KSh 30,000 for a deposit without knowing the unit
is real, the rent is what the borrower says, and the landlord exists. **That is exactly what your
directory is.** The framing is not "a free list" but *the only organisation in Kenya that can
underwrite a KSh 30,000 moving loan at the bottom of the market.* No Kenyan fintech does tenant
deposit lending today — the field is rent-collection software and rent-based credit scoring
([RentLynk](https://rentlynk.co.ke/); [RentScore / Nesti](https://startupmaphub.com/startups/fintech/kenya)) **[E]** —
so the slot is open.

This also fixes Q4: the banked reward for contributing becomes *better terms on your next moving
loan*, redeemed 30 months later, at the exact moment you need it. One mechanism, two problems.

## The three things this analysis says to change

**1. Make reporting unattributable, and pay for truth instead of volume.**
The verifier's expected payoff is negative because their deposit is hostage to the caretaker (Q5).
No reward you can afford fixes that; only structural anonymity does — no named reporter on a listing,
confirmations aggregated, volunteer roles rotated and detached from posting. Then condition every
reward on the **post-move-in audit**, so it attaches to a verified let rather than a post. That is
the only ground truth available to you, and the reputation literature says systems without it fail
(Q3).

**2. Replace the 6–12 hour head start with a banked, giftable credit — and make what it buys a
deposit loan.**
The head start is worth ~3% of face value at the moment you issue it (Q4). Give contributors three
expiring, named-recipient passes they can *give away*, plus credit that matures at their next move
into better terms on a moving loan. That accepts Q7's conclusion — the deposit is the real constraint
— without the platform touching money, because you are the underwriting data, not the lender.

**3. Publish vacancy freely. Never publish a browsable rent list.**
A 4% rent rise cancels every benefit this project delivers; Danish concrete produced 15–20% from
exactly this act; and thousands of small landlords are no protection when the real market is six
caretakers around one stage (Q2). Show a signed-in seeker prices on units they are considering; ship
no public, sortable, estate-level price feed; rate-limit; publish any index only in arrears and
coarse. **The seeker sees many prices. The landlord sees one.** This is the single decision that
determines whether the project helps tenants or hurts them.

---

### Where I am least confident

- Whether service charge is really split per occupant in this band (Q5) — 20 phone calls settles it.
- The true share of tenants paying a full month's commission (A5) — it moves every number in Q1 and Q7.
- Whether six caretakers around one stage coordinate on price or merely gossip (Q2). Highest-value
  unknown here, and answerable by asking.
- No Kenyan evidence exists on void length at this rent level, or on connectivity CAC. Both inferred.
